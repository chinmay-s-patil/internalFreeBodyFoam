\begin{abstract}
\noindent This report presents a verification, validation, and uncertainty quantification (VVUQ) assessment of the custom OpenFOAM solver \texttt{freeBody6DoFPimpleFoam}, developed for coupled three-dimensional fluid--structure interaction of freely falling and autorotating bodies in an accelerating reference frame. The solver couples transient incompressible Navier--Stokes equations with a non-conformal sliding Arbitrary Mesh Interface (\texttt{cyclicAMI}) and a 6-Degree-of-Freedom (6DoF) rigid-body integrator. Validation is conducted against the experimental benchmark of \textbf{Tinklenberg, Guala, and Coletti (J. Fluid Mech., vol. 962, A3, 2023; DOI: 10.1017/jfm.2023.209)}, examining a 2.02~mm diameter, 50~$\mu$m thick polyester disk falling in air across broadside and continuous tumbling regimes.

\medskip
\noindent \textbf{Current Status:} Code and solver verification are complete: dynamic coupling ($I_{xx} \Delta\omega / \Delta t$ vs.\ $M_x$) matches to $2.5 \times 10^{-6}$, mechanical energy conservation holds to $< 10^{-14}$ across $13,333$ steps ($\Delta t = 1.5 \times 10^{-5}$~s, $T = 0.20$~s), surface-traction boundary forces match OpenFOAM's standard \texttt{forces} function object to $2.1 \times 10^{-10}$, and rigid-body ODE convergence for angular velocity $\omega$ is verified at 4th order ($p = 3.991$) using RK4 on test functions (quaternion attitude update on $SO(3)$ is unverified for order when $\omega$ varies within a step). In production tumbling simulations across 13 completed revolutions ($29,715$ time steps), simulated tumbling kinematics are \textbf{consistent}: simulated Strouhal number ($St = 0.059$) matches the experimental peak ($St \approx 0.055$--$0.060$). However, tumbling frequency ($f_t = 30.52 \pm 0.17$~Hz) is also $\approx 16\%$ below the experimental mean ($\approx 36.4$~Hz); $St$ matches because velocity and frequency are both low by similar factors, pointing to an effective force or mass offset rather than an altered tumbling mode.

\medskip
\noindent Validation against the experimental mean terminal settling speed is partial: the simulated asymptotic velocity ($V_\infty \approx 1.02$~m/s) exhibits a $-17.1\%$ deficit relative to the experimental mean ($V_t = 1.23$~m/s). Direct 6DoF sensitivity tests bound mesh sensitivity at $-0.09\%$, time-step sensitivity at $+0.04\%$, and convection scheme sensitivity at $-0.75\%$ (each $\lesssim 1\%$; these are single-pair shifts, not a formal GCI). Specimen thickness scatter alone cannot explain the deficit (accounting for $-17\%$ speed solely via thickness would require about $-31\%$ mean thickness, $\approx 35\ \mu\text{m}$ vs.\ $50\ \mu\text{m}$); the cause of the $-17\%$ deficit remains unidentified and unresolved. Under ASME V\&V 20 guidelines, comparison error ($|E| = 0.206$~m/s) significantly exceeds the validation uncertainty of the mean ($u_{\mathrm{val}} \approx 0.018$~m/s). The simulation is \textbf{not validated} for mean terminal settling velocity within validation uncertainty.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Benchmark Specification}

The aerodynamics of freely falling planar bodies (disks, cards, plates) represents a canonical challenge in fluid dynamics due to non-linear coupling between separation-induced vortex shedding and multi-axis rigid-body kinematics \cite{varshney2012}. Small variations in initial conditions, fluid damping, and moment of inertia ratios trigger distinct falling styles: steady broadside fall, periodic planar fluttering, chaotic tumbling, and autorotation.

\subsection{Experimental Benchmark: Tinklenberg, Guala, and Coletti (2023)}
The experimental reference is the study by \textbf{Tinklenberg, Guala, and Coletti}, \emph{Thin disks falling in air}, published in the \emph{Journal of Fluid Mechanics}, volume 962, article A3 (2023), DOI: \href{https://doi.org/10.1017/jfm.2023.209}{10.1017/jfm.2023.209}. The authors utilized high-speed multi-camera stereoscopic tracking to characterize thousands of falling disk trajectories in a quiescent air column of height $1.5$~m and cross-section $0.4 \times 0.4$~m.

\subsection{Experimental Observations (Tinklenberg et al., Table 3)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mean Terminal Settling Velocity:} $V_t = 1.23 \pm 0.31$~m/s ($1\sigma$ population scatter across tracked drops; measurement uncertainty of the ensemble mean is $\pm 1.2\%$, or $\pm 0.015$~m/s).
    \item \textbf{Nominal Drag Coefficient Based on Projected Area:}
    \begin{equation}
    C_D = \frac{2 m g}{\rho_f A V_t^2} = 0.74 \quad \left(A = \frac{\pi}{4} D^2\right)
    \end{equation}
    Converting the instantaneous velocity spread $[0.92, 1.54]$~m/s yields an instantaneous drag range of $[0.47, 1.33]$.
    \item \textbf{Tumbling Rotation Rate:} Mean rotational rate magnitude $\langle |\Omega| \rangle = 228.7 \pm 65.9$~rad/s ($36.4 \pm 10.5$~Hz; PDF distribution peak at $\sim 33$~Hz).
    \item \textbf{Tumbling Strouhal Number:} Non-dimensional frequency distribution peaks sharply at $St = \frac{f_t D}{V_t} \approx 0.055$--$0.060$ (Figure~20b of \cite{tinklenberg2023}).
    \item \textbf{Trajectory Inclination:} Trajectory inclination angle $\psi$ relative to vertical exhibits a modal peak at $\approx 10.5^\circ$ with excursions up to $40^\circ$ (Figure~10b of \cite{tinklenberg2023}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Mathematical Formulation and Numerical Implementation}

\subsection{Incompressible Navier--Stokes in an Accelerating Reference Frame}
Simulating large vertical displacements in a laboratory frame is computationally prohibitive. The \texttt{freeBody6DoFPimpleFoam} solver implements an accelerating reference frame fixed to the translating center of mass of the disk:
\begin{equation}
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u} = -\frac{1}{\rho_f} \nabla p_{\mathrm{rgh}} + \nu \nabla^2 \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{frame}} - \mathbf{g} \left(1 - \frac{\rho_\infty}{\rho_f}\right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{U} - \mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{frame}}$ is the relative flow velocity, $\mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{frame}} = \frac{d\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{frame}}}{dt}$ is the frame acceleration, and $p_{\mathrm{rgh}} = p - \rho_f (\mathbf{g} - \mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{frame}}) \cdot \mathbf{x}$ is the dynamic pseudo-pressure.

\subsection{Coupled 6DoF Dynamic Equations}
Translational motion follows Newton's second law:
\begin{equation}
m \frac{d\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{body}}}{dt} = m \mathbf{g} + \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{aero}} \implies \mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{frame}} = \mathbf{g} + \frac{1}{m} \int_{\partial \Omega_{\mathrm{disk}}} \left( -p \mathbf{n} + \boldsymbol{\tau} \cdot \mathbf{n} \right) dS
\end{equation}
Rotational dynamics are solved in body-fixed principal coordinates:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{body}} \frac{d\boldsymbol{\omega}}{dt} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times (\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{body}} \boldsymbol{\omega}) = \mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{aero}} = \int_{\partial \Omega_{\mathrm{disk}}} (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_c) \times \left( -p \mathbf{n} + \boldsymbol{\tau} \cdot \mathbf{n} \right) dS
\end{equation}
Orientation is integrated in 4D quaternion space $\mathbf{q} = (q_0, q_1, q_2, q_3)$ to eliminate gimbal lock:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\mathbf{q}}{dt} = \frac{1}{2} \mathbf{q} \otimes \boldsymbol{\omega}_q, \quad \boldsymbol{\omega}_q = (0, \omega_x, \omega_y, \omega_z)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Mesh Architecture and Arbitrary Mesh Interface (AMI)}
The domain comprises two concentric regions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Inner Rotating Zone:} A sphere of diameter $4.04$~mm ($2 D$) enclosing the disk. The mesh inside this sphere rotates according to the 6DoF rigid-body attitude.
    \item \textbf{Outer Stationary Zone:} A block domain ($30 \times 50 \times 30$~mm, or $15 D \times 25 D \times 15 D$) connected to the rotating sphere via OpenFOAM's sliding \texttt{cyclicAMI} interface.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Asymptotic Limit-Cycle Extrapolation}
Fitting an exponential relaxation $V_y(t) = V_\infty + (V_0 - V_\infty) e^{-(t - t_0)/\tau}$ across Revs 6--13 yields:
\begin{equation}
V_\infty = 1.0159\ \mathrm{m/s}, \quad \tau = 97.4\ \mathrm{ms} \quad (R^2 = 0.994)
\end{equation}
Aitken $\Delta^2$ sequence extrapolation on the mean cycle velocities yields $V_\infty = 1.0236$~m/s. Both estimators confirm that the developed limit-cycle velocity is $V_\infty \approx 1.02$~m/s, establishing a \textbf{$-17.1\%$ asymptotic deficit} relative to the experimental mean ($1.23$~m/s).

\section{Fixed Broadside Disk Aerodynamic Diagnostics \texorpdfstring{($Re = 100$ and $Re = 140$)}{(Re = 100 and Re = 140)}}

To isolate fluid-dynamic discretization error from coupled 6DoF kinematic degrees of freedom, fixed broadside disk simulations were conducted using \texttt{make\_fixed\_disk.sh} under uniform inflow with frozen frame velocity and attitude ($\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{frame}} = \mathbf{0}$, $\boldsymbol{\omega} = \mathbf{0}$, $\mathbf{g} = \mathbf{0}$).

\subsection{Wake Instability Regimes and Theoretical Linear Stability}
Per the global linear stability analysis of Meliga et al. (2009) \cite{meliga2009} and Fabre et al. (2008) \cite{fabre2008}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Subcritical Axisymmetric Regime ($Re < Re_{c1} \approx 116.9$):} At $Re = 100$, the wake is strictly steady and axisymmetric ($\sigma < 0$, $F_{\mathrm{lat}} \to 0$). Secondary literature citations (commonly attributed to Shenoy \& Kleinstreuer 2008 \cite{shenoy2008} for $h/D = 0.1$, and Roos \& Willmarth 1971 \cite{roos1971} cited via Tian \cite{tian2019}) report steady broadside drag coefficients in the range:
    \begin{equation}
    C_D(Re = 100) \approx 1.15 \text{ -- } 1.25 \quad \text{[unverified secondary citation]}
    \end{equation}
    This range remains unverified pending direct primary audit of Shenoy \& Kleinstreuer's tabulated data.
    \item \textbf{Supercritical Bifurcation Regimes ($Re > Re_{c1} \approx 116.9$ and $Re > Re_{c2} \approx 125.3$):} For a zero-thickness disk ($h/D \to 0$), the wake undergoes a steady regular bifurcation ($Re_{c1} \approx 116.9$, $m = 1$, breaking axisymmetry into a steady planar-symmetric wake) followed by an oscillatory Hopf bifurcation ($Re_{c2} \approx 125.3$, periodic hairpin vortex shedding). At $Re = 140$, the wake is supercritical to both thresholds. In unseeded simulations, the flow develops the steady planar $m = 1$ deflection first (producing a non-zero lateral force $F_{\mathrm{lat}} \neq 0$ with locked spatial azimuth), with unsteady oscillatory vortex shedding not yet observed over the simulated convective duration.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Physical Evaluation and Critical Interpretations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Supercritical Lateral Instability \& Steady Planar Mode ($Re = 140$):} On the refined mesh (\texttt{wakeFine}), lateral forces grew exponentially from $|F_{\mathrm{lat}}|/F_y = 6.7 \times 10^{-5}$ ($1.56 \times 10^{-10}$~N) at $50$~ms to $1.80 \times 10^{-3}$ ($4.15 \times 10^{-9}$~N) at $160$~ms, exhibiting an exponential growth rate of $\sigma = +30.0\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}$ ($\sigma^* = \sigma D / V = +0.0582$ over $80$--$160$~ms, GROWING). Crucially, the force azimuth angle remained strictly constant at $\phi = \operatorname{atan2}(F_z, F_x) = +145.5^\circ \pm 0.15^\circ$ across the run, with zero rotation or sign reversal. This demonstrates that the amplifying instability is the \textbf{steady regular $m = 1$ planar bifurcation mode} (threshold $Re_{c1} \approx 116.9$), rather than oscillatory vortex shedding ($Re_{c2} \approx 125.3$), which has not yet manifested from unseeded startup flow. On the baseline mesh, following an initial startup zero-crossing transient between $30$ and $50$~ms ($-99.7^\circ \to +98.9^\circ$), the azimuth locked at $\phi = +95.5^\circ \pm 0.08^\circ$ with a $2.9\times$ slower growth rate of $\sigma = +10.5\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}$ ($\sigma^* = +0.0204$ over $80$--$160$~ms; $+8.5\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}$ over $80$--$200$~ms). The two meshes differ in growth rate by $2.9\times$, reflecting differing levels of numerical diffusion across the sliding AMI boundary. The initial lateral force ($\sim 1.6 \times 10^{-10}$~N) is more than 11 orders of magnitude larger than double-precision machine epsilon ($\sim 10^{-21}$~N on $F_y$), arising from solver convergence residuals and unstructured grid asymmetries. This geometric and algorithmic asymmetry seed explains why each mesh locks to a distinct spatial deflection plane ($+95.5^\circ$ vs.\ $+145.5^\circ$).
    \item \textbf{Broadside Drag Refinement \& Kinematic Scaling ($Re = 140$):} Evaluated over the identical window $t \in [96.0, 160.0]$~ms, $C_D$ is $1.1523$ on baseline (drift $-0.01\% / 100$~ms) and $1.1125$ on \texttt{wakeFine} (drift $-0.02\% / 100$~ms), confirming that the $-3.45\%$ drag difference survives the initial startup transient. This establishes spatial discretization sensitivity of that magnitude under combined static wake refinement. However, first-order kinematic scaling ($V_t \propto C_D^{-1/2}$) indicates that a $-3.45\%$ broadside drag reduction yields only a $+1.73\% \approx +1.8\%$ increase in settling speed ($1.020 \to 1.038$~m/s), recovering $\approx 1.5$ percentage points of the $-17\%$ deficit (moving from $-17.1\%$ to $-15.6\%$). Thus, static wake refinement alone accounts for only $\sim 10\%$ of the gap.
    \item \textbf{Domain \& Far-Field Boundary Clearance ($Re = 140$):} To test whether domain blockage and boundary proximity in the baseline $15D \times 25D \times 15D$ box contribute to broadside drag elevation, a fixed-disk simulation was executed in a $1.5\times$ expanded domain ($45\text{ mm} \times 75\text{ mm} \times 45\text{ mm}$, $\approx 22.5D \times 37.5D \times 22.5D$, 267k cells). At $t = 80$~ms, the broadside drag coefficient is $C_D = 1.15244$ compared to $1.15267$ on the baseline domain (a shift of only $\mathbf{-0.019\%}$). Across the time history ($t \in [10, 80]$~ms), the shift remains within $\pm 0.03\%$. Far-field domain blockage is thus completely negligible and does not account for the $-17\%$ settling speed deficit.
    \item \textbf{Subcritical Benchmark ($Re = 100$):} Both the baseline mesh ($11,544$ steps, $t = 250.0$~ms, $3.71$ flow-throughs) and the refined wake mesh (\texttt{wakeFine}, $9,664$ steps, $t = 210.0$~ms, $3.12$ flow-throughs) cleanly satisfied both criteria of the convergence gate ($\ge 3.0$ flow-throughs [\textbf{PASS}]; linear $C_D$ drift $< 1.0\% / 100$~ms [\textbf{PASS}]). Evaluated over the identical window $t \in [126.0, 210.0]$~ms ($84.0$~ms duration), broadside drag is strictly plateaued at $C_D = 1.2992$ on baseline and $C_D = 1.2738$ on \texttt{wakeFine}, establishing a \textbf{$-1.96\%$ spatial discretization shift}. Lateral forces remain strictly suppressed at $\langle |F_{\mathrm{lat}}| \rangle / F_y = 2.65 \times 10^{-7}$ ($\sim 10^{-13}$~N amplitude, post-startup modal decay at $\sigma = -12.9\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}$), confirming that the numerical solver preserves the steady axisymmetric state without artificial symmetry breaking on both grids.
\end{itemize}

\section{Dynamic Limit-Cycle Restarts and Temporal Convergence}

To test numerical convergence of the autorotating limit cycle without recomputing the computationally intensive $300$~ms startup transient, a dynamic restart methodology was established using \texttt{make\_restart\_variants.sh}.

\subsection{Restart Methodology and Equilibrium Velocity Estimator}
At $t = 0.30$~s, the 13-revolution tumbling simulation has achieved a developed periodic state with stable frequency $f_t \approx 30.5$~Hz. Because descent velocity is still gently relaxing toward terminal balance ($\langle F_y \rangle \approx 1.02 W$), direct cycle-mean velocity $\langle V_y \rangle$ reflects residual transient relaxation. To isolate true asymptotic behavior over short evaluation windows, an equilibrium velocity estimator is defined based on quasi-steady quadratic drag ($F \propto V^2$):
\begin{equation}
V_{\mathrm{eq,est}} = \frac{\langle V_y \rangle}{\sqrt{\langle F_y \rangle / W}}
\end{equation}
On the full 13-revolution run, $V_{\mathrm{eq,est}}$ evaluates to $1.0252$~m/s, matching the formal Aitken $\Delta^2$ asymptotic extrapolation ($1.0236$~m/s) to within $0.15\%$. Evaluated across complete revolution boundaries via \texttt{compare\_variants.py}, $V_{\mathrm{eq,est}}$ provides an unbiased metric of limit-cycle settling speed.

\subsection{Control Benchmark and Temporal Refinement Cases}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Control Restart (\texttt{restart\_base}):} Restarts from $t = 0.30$~s and runs for 4 full revolutions to $t = 0.44$~s using identical baseline numerics ($\mathrm{Co}_{\mathrm{max}} \le 1.5$, $\Delta t \approx 15\ \mu$s, central-blended LUST convection).
    \item \textbf{Temporal Refinement (\texttt{restart\_dtHalf}):} Halves the time step by enforcing $\mathrm{Co}_{\mathrm{max}} \le 0.75$ ($\Delta t \approx 7.27\ \mu$s, maintaining standard \texttt{nOuterCorrectors 1}), directly isolating temporal discretization error.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Analysis of the \texorpdfstring{$-17\%$}{-17\%} Settling Velocity Deficit}

The baseline tumbling simulation yields an asymptotic descent velocity of $V_\infty \approx 1.02$~m/s, representing a $-17.1\%$ deficit relative to the experimental mean terminal velocity $V_t = 1.23$~m/s. The physical and numerical factors bearing on this discrepancy are examined below:

\subsection{Static Wake Discretization Error}
The baseline grid (213k cells) possesses an outer background cell size of $1.25$~mm ($0.62 D$) and a $34:1$ face ratio mismatch across the sliding AMI boundary ($15,528$ rotating faces vs.\ $456$ static faces). Static wake refinement (\texttt{wakeFine}, 487k cells, $6,984$ static AMI faces) reduces broadside drag by $-3.45\%$ at $Re = 140$ and $-1.96\%$ at $Re = 100$. By first-order momentum scaling ($V_t \propto C_D^{-1/2}$):
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Delta V_t}{V_t} \approx -\frac{1}{2} \frac{\Delta C_D}{C_D} = -\frac{1}{2}(-3.45\%) = +1.73\% \approx +1.8\%
\end{equation}
This proxy suggested an increase in settling velocity from $1.020 \to 1.038$~m/s, recovering approximately $1.5$ percentage points of the $17$-point deficit.

\subsection{Kinematic Consistency of the Tumbling Mode}
Crucially, the simulated Strouhal number $St = f_t D / V_t = 0.059$ agrees with the experimental mode ($0.055$--$0.060$). Descent speed is $17\%$ low ($1.02$ vs.\ $1.23$~m/s), and tumbling frequency is $16\%$ below the experimental mean ($30.5$ vs.\ $36.4$~Hz). Because $f_t / V_t$ is preserved, the tumbling kinematics scale consistently with the lower descent velocity. This indicates that the simulated disk is in the correct tumbling regime, and points to a force-level or mass offset rather than a fundamentally altered tumbling pattern.

\subsection{Physical Specimen Batch Thickness vs.\ Mean Uncertainty}
In Tinklenberg et al. (2023), Table~1 specifies the measured mean disk thickness as $h = 50 \pm 7.5\ \mu$m. This $\pm 7.5\ \mu$m ($\pm 15\%$) represents the standard deviation across the manufactured disk batch, reflecting specimen-to-specimen variation. Because the experimental terminal velocity $V_t = 1.23$~m/s is an ensemble mean averaged over thousands of tracked drops, the appropriate input uncertainty on the mean thickness is the standard error of the mean ($\sigma_h / \sqrt{N}$), which is small ($< 1\%$). Furthermore, because $V_t \propto \sqrt{h}$ at fixed drag coefficient, accounting for a $-17\%$ velocity deficit solely through thickness would require a $-31\%$ deficit in mean thickness ($\approx 34.5\ \mu\text{m}$ instead of $50\ \mu\text{m}$). Specimen thickness scatter around the mean shifts the mean velocity by only a fraction of a percent, and caliper measurements on stacks confirmed the $50\ \mu\text{m}$ nominal mean. Therefore, specimen thickness scatter alone cannot explain the deficit, and the underlying cause remains unidentified.

\subsection{Instantaneous Population Envelope vs.\ Mean Velocity}
The experimental report of $V_t = 1.23 \pm 0.31$~m/s represents the instantaneous velocity probability density function across thousands of tracked drops, with a $1\sigma$ spread of $[0.92, 1.54]$~m/s (measurement uncertainty of the ensemble mean is $\pm 1.2\%$, or $\pm 0.015$~m/s). The simulated asymptotic limit-cycle velocity ($1.02$~m/s) lies at:
\begin{equation}
Z = \frac{1.02 - 1.23}{0.31} = -0.68\sigma
\end{equation}
The simulated settling speed falls within the physical experimental observation envelope, confirming that disks frequently descended at $1.02$~m/s in the experimental facility. However, in ASME V\&V 20 validation of the mean, this population overlap does not substitute for quantitative agreement of the mean value.

\section{ASME V\&V 20 Verification and Validation Assessment}

In accordance with the ASME V\&V 20-2009 standard \cite{asme2009}, the validation comparison error between the simulation value $S$ and the experimental mean $D$ is:
\begin{equation}
E = S - D = 1.024\ \mathrm{m/s} - 1.230\ \mathrm{m/s} = -0.206\ \mathrm{m/s} \quad (-16.7\%)
\end{equation}
The validation uncertainty $u_{\mathrm{val}}$ combines numerical simulation uncertainty $u_{\mathrm{num}}$, input parameter uncertainty $u_{\mathrm{input}}$, and experimental measurement uncertainty $u_{\mathrm{exp}}$:
\begin{equation}
u_{\mathrm{val}} = \sqrt{u_{\mathrm{num}}^2 + u_{\mathrm{input}}^2 + u_{\mathrm{exp}}^2}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Numerical Uncertainty ($u_{\mathrm{num}}$):} Directly evaluated from the suite of dynamic 6DoF sensitivity tests: direct mesh sensitivity $u_{\mathrm{mesh}} = |-0.09\%| \approx 0.0009$~m/s, time-step sensitivity $u_{\Delta t} = |+0.04\%| \approx 0.0004$~m/s, and convection scheme sensitivity $u_{\mathrm{scheme}} = |-0.75\%| \approx 0.0077$~m/s:
    \begin{equation}
    u_{\mathrm{num}} = \sqrt{u_{\mathrm{mesh}}^2 + u_{\Delta t}^2 + u_{\mathrm{scheme}}^2} \approx 0.0078\ \mathrm{m/s} \quad (0.76\%)
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Input Parameter Uncertainty ($u_{\mathrm{input}}$):} Uncertainty in the experimental mean disk thickness is small ($< 1\%$), giving $u_{\mathrm{input}} \ll 0.01$~m/s (taken conservatively as $0.005$~m/s), assuming nominal mean thickness ($h = 50\ \mu$m) is unbiased.
    \item \textbf{Experimental Measurement Uncertainty ($u_{\mathrm{exp}}$):} The stereoscopic tracking measurement error of the ensemble mean velocity is $u_{\mathrm{exp}} = \pm 1.2\%$ ($0.015$~m/s).
\end{itemize}
Combining these uncertainties:
\begin{equation}
u_{\mathrm{val}} = \sqrt{(0.0078)^2 + (0.0050)^2 + (0.0150)^2} \approx 0.0176\ \mathrm{m/s} \quad (1.4\%)
\end{equation}
Comparing the comparison error to validation uncertainty:
\begin{equation}
|E| = 0.206\ \mathrm{m/s} \approx 11.7\ u_{\mathrm{val}} \gg u_{\mathrm{val}}
\end{equation}
\textbf{ASME V\&V 20 Verdict:} Because $|E| \gg u_{\mathrm{val}}$, the modeling error $\delta_{\mathrm{model}} \approx E \pm u_{\mathrm{val}} = -0.206 \pm 0.018$~m/s is statistically significant. The simulation is \textbf{not validated} for mean terminal settling velocity within validation uncertainty. Because sensitivity tests bound individual parameter shifts at $\lesssim 1\%$ (single-pair tests, no formal GCI), numerical discretization sensitivities alone are unlikely to account for the full $-17\%$ deficit. The discrepancy remains an unresolved physical or flow modeling issue.
